A MODEL. ELEVEN PRODUCTS. ONE BIG QUESTION.

From first buyers.
To the mainstream.

Why do some new products spread slowly at first, then take off, and eventually lose momentum—and can we predict when sales will peak? Bass explains this pattern through two sources of first-time adoption: people buying independently and people influenced by earlier buyers, both acting on a shrinking pool of potential customers. The paper turns that idea into a growth model and compares its predictions with sales data for eleven consumer durables.

The first purchase is the event.

Imagine a new category of durable product—such as a home freezer. Some households buy without waiting for many others to adopt. As ownership spreads, social influence can accelerate adoption. Eventually fewer first-time buyers remain.

Bass turns this story into a simple equation and asks whether it describes historical growth. The model concerns initial purchases of a product category, rather than brand choice, repeat purchases, or replacement sales.

The practical aim is to anticipate when demand peaks, so a manufacturer can plan production capacity instead of extrapolating early growth forever.

A falling first-purchase curve does not imply that all sales disappear. Replacement purchases are outside this model.

A small dictionary

m · market potential
The total number of eventual first purchases over the modeled product life.
F(t) · fraction adopted
The share of that potential market that has already bought; Y(t) = mF(t) is the cumulative count.
S(t) · first-purchase rate
New first purchases per unit time. It is the slope of cumulative adoption, not the cumulative total.
p and q · two pressures
p is the innovation coefficient; q scales the influence of previous adoption. Both depend on the chosen time unit.
Hazard p + qF(t)
The instantaneous adoption rate conditional on not yet adopting. It is not itself a one-year probability.
