What is a polar body, and why care about its volume?NEW TO THIS PAPER? START HERE.

A second shape made from constraints.

A convex body is a solid shape with no inward dents: the segment joining any two of its points stays inside. Here it is symmetric about the origin—whenever x belongs to it, so does −x.

Its polar K° consists of all vectors y whose dot product with every x in K is at most 1. A long direction in K imposes a tight constraint in that direction on K°. The polar of a square is a diamond; the polar of the unit disk is the same disk.

K° = {y : ⟨x,y⟩ ≤ 1 for every x ∈ K}

The product |K||K°| survives every invertible linear change of coordinates. It measures a geometric relationship that stretching or shearing alone cannot improve. The paper establishes the proposed sharp minimum in every dimension and identifies all minimizers.

What the pictures teach

Volume product
Area × area in two dimensions; volume × volume in higher dimensions. The square–diamond pair has product 8. The unit disk has product π² ≈ 9.8696.
Linear invariance
If K becomes TK, its polar becomes T⁻ᵀK°. One volume scales by |det T|, the other by its reciprocal.
Hanner body
Start with intervals. Repeatedly take products or convex-hull joins in complementary dimensions, then allow invertible linear transformations. These are the paper’s equality shapes.

INSIDE THE KEY CONSTRUCTIONWhy introduce a lens from complex analysis?

Turn uniform angles into uniform heights.

The paper’s conformal map sends the disk to a lens. A point sampled uniformly around the original circle has a uniform imaginary coordinate after mapping. That exact distribution lets a probability sum become a real volume integral.

The ordinary circle does not have this property: its vertical coordinate spends more angular time near the top and bottom. Move the angle to compare the coordinates.

The lens’s horizontal coordinate uses 500 terms of equation (8); its height uses the exact boundary law. The construction follows Lemma 3.1.
