A PAPER IN TWO THEOREMS

Order emerges.
At what scale?

Imagine heat flowing through a composite material with tiny, randomly arranged patches—some conduct heat easily, while others act as insulation. At the microscopic scale, we can distinguish these patches and their different effects on heat flow. At the macroscopic scale, we observe a much larger region containing many patches. Can the whole mixture then be described approximately as one uniform material with an effective conductivity? The challenge is especially sharp in high contrast, when the differences in conductivity are very large. This paper asks how far we must zoom out for that simpler description to become reliable. Under its mathematical assumptions, it bounds a sufficient observation scale and quantifies how the effective description approaches a fixed large-scale limit.

Homogenization at a polynomial scale in high contrast

The setting, the question & the discoveryIntroduction

01 · THE PHYSICAL QUESTION

When can an uneven material act like a uniform conductor?

Imagine a composite containing randomly arranged regions that conduct electricity well and regions that conduct poorly. Current travels easily along connected conducting regions, but poorly conducting gaps create bottlenecks. Both the conductivity differences and the arrangement matter.

Across a large enough sample, we hope to predict the overall current using one effective conductivity, instead of tracking every patch. This replacement is called homogenization. The paper asks: how large must the observation scale be, especially when the conductivity differences are enormous?

02 · WHAT THE PAPER FINDS

A polynomial scale is enough.

Under the paper’s assumptions, a sufficient starting scale grows at most as a fixed power of the parameters measuring the material’s contrast and statistical tails. Beyond an appropriate starting scale, the authors also quantify how the effective description converges as we zoom out further.

This turns “the material eventually has a predictable large-scale response” into a quantitative guarantee about when that description becomes accurate and how it improves.

03 · FROM THE MATERIAL TO THE MATHEMATICS

Give each part of the picture a name.

At position x, let u(x) be the electric potential and a(x) the local conductivity matrix, which can account for different conductivities in different directions. The potential gradient drives the current j(x). In a steady state with no internal sources, current is conserved:

Local conduction law
j(x) = −a(x)∇u(x)
Conservation of current
−∇ · (a(x)∇u(x)) = 0

The large-scale model replaces a(x) with a constant matrix ā. To see what the scale bound means in a familiar case, suppose conductivity has a positive lower bound λ and upper bound Λ. Their ratio R = Λ/λ measures contrast: a larger R allows greater differences. At fixed accuracy, the paper bounds the characteristic starting length by C(2 + R)C, with probabilistic control of sample-to-sample variation. Lengths are measured in units of the distance beyond which the material’s randomness is independent.

Connect this picture to Theorems A and B

The general result also covers materials without global conductivity bounds. It uses a reference coarse-contrast parameter Π and a statistical-tail parameter KΨS. These replace R in the general starting-scale bound.

At observation length 3m, the paper compares two energy descriptions averaged over the random material. Their relative mismatch is Θm − 1. Theorem A makes this mismatch small after a polynomially bounded length; Theorem B proves a power-law rate of convergence to the limiting effective description. Theorems C and D translate that control into estimates for solutions of the equation.

04 · WHAT CHANGES FROM EARLIER WORK

The exponent no longer grows with contrast.

Earlier quantitative theory estimated homogenization errors for fixed contrast, but tracking its constants gave at least exponential scale bounds in a power of R. The authors’ preceding work, [AK25], brought that guarantee down to a bound with a logarithmically growing exponent. This paper answers the remaining question: can a fixed exponent suffice?

Previous work · [AK25]
(1 + R)C log(1 + R)

The exponent grows as the contrast increases.

This paper
C(2 + R)C

The exponent stays fixed at fixed accuracy and dimension.

These are upper-bound forms for a sufficient scale. C denotes constants, not a single shared numerical value. The general theorem uses coarse contrast and statistical-tail parameters.

05 · THE KEY PROOF STRATEGIES

Prepare once. Carry the estimates forward. Close the gap.

The authors need more than a stable average: two complementary energy descriptions must agree. Their earlier argument paid a logarithmic preparation cost at each of logarithmically many stages. Here they retain control through changes of scale and geometry, finding a suitable interval in only logarithmically many scale levels overall. Since physical length is exponential in the scale index, this gives a polynomial length bound.

  1. Separate fluctuations from changes in the mean.

    Separated child blocks provide independent random matrices whose average concentrates. The parent matrix is bounded above by that average through subadditivity; it is not equal to it. The difference is positive semidefinite, and its expectation is the decrease in the mean matrix. The proof controls both effects, retaining earlier errors in weighted histories so that a change of grid does not discard them.

    §1.3; §2.1, (2.7)–(2.9); §3

  2. Adapt the geometry gradually.

    Elongated blocks balance the gradient and flux estimates when the material responds differently in different directions. After a shape update, a Whitney partition fills most of a new block with large old-grid blocks, using smaller blocks near its boundary. Their decreasing volume offsets worsening fine-scale bounds because γ < 1. Updates transfer existing control; they are scheduled when the incoming errors and mean variation are small.

    §1.3; §2.1, (2.1); §4

  3. Count error, shape progress and determinant loss together.

    A logarithmic potential tracks errors and drift together with the distance to a preferred block shape. On a fixed grid, a decrease in the log determinant pays for changes in the mean. A grid change can increase that determinant, so the argument also controls those increases and compensates for them through geometric progress. Summing this accounting bounds the entire preparation by C log(2 + Π) scale levels, after initialization; the tail parameter enters the final scale bound.

    §1.3; Proposition 2.1 and Proposition 5.2

  4. Compare the two optimizer energies on the selected interval.

    Small mean variation makes optimizers on large and small blocks close in energy. Fluctuation estimates control their averaged centered gradients and fluxes; PDE estimates turn that control into an energy comparison. Adapted geometry keeps the resulting bound linear in the still-unknown contrast, so a small multiple of that contrast can be absorbed to close the gap. The proof then transfers the annealed matrix estimate to larger Euclidean cubes.

    §1.3; Proposition 5.3; §5

  5. Use a separate iteration to obtain a convergence rate.

    Entry into a fixed small-gap regime supplies the starting point for the small-contrast iteration of [AK25]. This gives algebraic convergence to a deterministic effective matrix. The later PDE estimates combine that convergence with deterministic estimates from the earlier work.

    §1.2; §6

What is new: the proof preserves and accounts for estimates across the whole preparation, including temporary increases in errors. It closes the primal–dual gap on a selected interval, rather than requiring that gap to contract at every intermediate step.

06 · KEY MATHEMATICAL DEFINITIONS

The quantities behind the strategy.

Each definition below names something the argument needs to measure: local response, averaged energy, mismatch, or progress across scales. All matrix norms below are operator norms; X ≤ Y means Y − X is positive semidefinite.

1 · Local conductivity and observation length

The field a(x) describes the local response. Its symmetric part s measures positive energy; k is its skew-symmetric part. The cube index m counts zoom levels: one step multiplies its side length by three.

\[s=\tfrac12(a+a^{\mathsf T}),\qquad k=\tfrac12(a-a^{\mathsf T}),\qquad \square_m=(-3^m/2,3^m/2)^d.\]

In dimension d ≥ 2, s is positive definite almost everywhere and s, s⁻¹, kᵀs⁻¹k are locally essentially bounded. The stochastic assumptions are stationarity, unit-range dependence and coarse ellipticity above a random scale; see P1–P3 below.

§1.1, (1.2) ↗

2 · A block’s energy response and its ensemble mean

The variational matrix A(U; a) packages the energy response on a region U with gradient and flux data. It is a symmetric positive definite 2d × 2d matrix. Averaging over random materials gives the annealed matrix, written with a bar:

\[\overline{\mathbf A}(U)=\mathbb E[\mathbf A(U;a)]=\begin{pmatrix}\bar s+\bar k^{\mathsf T}\bar s_*^{-1}\bar k&-\bar k^{\mathsf T}\bar s_*^{-1}\\-\bar s_*^{-1}\bar k&\bar s_*^{-1}\end{pmatrix}(U).\]

The barred blocks are parameters of the averaged variational matrix, not the pointwise s and k above. The paper defines the underlying variational problem by reference to [AK25, (2.13)–(2.14)].

(1.7); definition on p.3 ↗

3 · Fixed reference contrast Π

The assumption supplies an upper reference block E₀. Its contrast Π compares upper and lower bounds in fixed Euclidean coordinates. Minimizing over a constant skew-symmetric h removes a part that does not affect the PDE.

\[\mathbf E_0=\begin{pmatrix}s_0+k_0^{\mathsf T}s_{*,0}^{-1}k_0&-k_0^{\mathsf T}s_{*,0}^{-1}\\-s_{*,0}^{-1}k_0&s_{*,0}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad s_0,s_{*,0}>0.\] \[\Pi=\lVert s_{*,0}^{-1}\rVert\min_{h^{\mathsf T}=-h}\lVert s_0+(k_0-h)^{\mathsf T}s_{*,0}^{-1}(k_0-h)\rVert\ge1.\]

Π is fixed by the assumptions. It can be large even for a constant, strongly anisotropic material.

(1.5)–(1.6) ↗

4 · Scale-dependent mismatch Θₘ − 1

The proof asks whether the primal and dual energy descriptions agree at the current scale. It measures this using the barred blocks evaluated on the cube □ₘ:

\[\Theta_m=\min_{h^{\mathsf T}=-h}\left\lVert\bar s_*^{-1/2}\bigl(\bar s+(\bar k-h)^{\mathsf T}\bar s_*^{-1}(\bar k-h)\bigr)\bar s_*^{-1/2}\right\rVert(\square_m).\]

Agreement means Θₘ approaches 1. A constant coefficient has Θₘ = 1 even if its directional conductivities differ greatly: this quantity measures primal–dual mismatch, not isotropy.

(1.8) ↗

5 · The entry scale being bounded

Choose a tolerated mismatch σ. The result gives a sufficient cube size beyond which the annealed mismatch stays below that tolerance. The tail-growth constant KΨS comes from P3.

\[m_{\mathrm{ent}}=\left\lceil C(\sigma,d,\gamma)\log_3(2+\Pi K_{\Psi_S})\right\rceil,\qquad\Theta_m\le1+\sigma\quad(m\ge m_{\mathrm{ent}}).\] \[3^{m_{\mathrm{ent}}}\le3(2+\Pi K_{\Psi_S})^{C(\sigma,d,\gamma)},\qquad 0<\sigma\le1.\]

Thus logarithmically many zoom levels mean a polynomial physical length. C depends on tolerance, dimension and γ; the material law enters this length bound through Π and KΨS.

(1.9) and its length bound ↗
The proof’s accounting quantities: adapted blocks, fluctuations and determinant loss

An invertible rounded matrix q changes the shape of the measuring blocks. Define the adapted cube and its mean response by

\[\Diamond_j^q=q\square_j,\qquad \overline{\mathbf A}_{j,q}=\mathbb E[\mathbf A(\Diamond_j^q;a)].\]

To compare an earlier scale j with a later scale k, normalize by the later mean. P measures the earlier mean; V measures the random deviation from that mean:

\[P_{j,k}^q=\overline{\mathbf A}_{k,q}^{-1/2}\overline{\mathbf A}_{j,q}\overline{\mathbf A}_{k,q}^{-1/2},\] \[V_{j,k}^q(z)=\overline{\mathbf A}_{k,q}^{-1/2}\bigl(\mathbf A(z+\Diamond_j^q;a)-\overline{\mathbf A}_{j,q}\bigr)\overline{\mathbf A}_{k,q}^{-1/2}.\]

On a fixed admissible grid, above its starting scale j*, subadditivity orders the means. Their log-determinant loss is nonnegative and adds across adjacent scale intervals:

\[\Delta_{j,k}^q=\log\det\overline{\mathbf A}_{j,q}-\log\det\overline{\mathbf A}_{k,q}\ge0,\qquad j_*\le j\le k.\]

The weighted histories in (2.7)–(2.9) retain earlier fluctuations, mean differences and mean increments with decreasing weights. This is the information needed to compare grids that intersect blocks at many different scales. Determinant monotonicity here applies to a fixed grid; a geometry change requires a separate comparison.

§2.1, (2.1)–(2.9), pp.12–13 ↗
Assumptions & exact notation · P1–P3

Work in dimension d ≥ 2. The symmetric part of a is positive definite almost everywhere. The local bounds (1.2) apply to s, s⁻¹ and kᵀs⁻¹k; global uniform ellipticity or symmetry of a is not assumed. The following is a reading guide to the precise assumptions on pp.2–4.

P1 · Stationarity
The law is invariant under integer translations.
P2 · Unit range of dependence
Coefficient information in sets at distance at least 1 is independent.
P3 · Random-scale coarse ellipticity
Fix a positive definite reference block E₀, γ ∈ [0,1), an increasing tail function ΨS and KΨ > 1. A random S satisfies P[S > t] ≤ ΨS(t)⁻¹ and tΨS(t) ≤ ΨS(KΨt) for t ≥ 1. Almost surely, for every integer m with 3m ≥ S, the random variational block obeys A(y+□k; a) ≤ 3γ(m−k)E₀ for k ≤ m and y ∈ 3kℤd ∩ □m.
Standard cubes
□m = (−3m/2, 3m/2)d. The scale index m is logarithmic in physical length.
Two different contrasts
Π ≥ 1 measures the reference bound in fixed coordinates, defined in (1.6). Θm ≥ 1 measures annealed primal–dual mismatch, defined in (1.8). Even a constant anisotropic coefficient can have large Π but Θm = 1.
Matrix order
X ≤ Y means Y − X is positive semidefinite. A∞ here denotes the paper’s limiting 2d × 2d block matrix; ā is its associated d × d effective coefficient.
