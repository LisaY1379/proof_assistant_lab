1. Definitional unfolding: Expand notions such as boundedness, set inclusion, local extrema, Riemann sums, and auxiliary functions into their underlying quantified statements, then simplify.
2. Metric and filter characterization: Replace continuity, convergence, Cauchy conditions, accumulation points, and eventual properties by their standard neighborhood or ε–δ formulations, including punctured-neighborhood and `atTop` descriptions.
3. Canonical rewriting and normalization: Identify real distance with absolute difference, real norm with absolute value, difference quotients with slopes, interval interiors with open intervals, and scalar or formal-series norms with their concrete forms.
4. Logical decomposition and case analysis: Split conjunctions, disjunctions, order cases, endpoint cases, and degenerate intervals; use contradiction or contraposition when ruling out alternatives or proving nonexistence.
5. Explicit witness and test-point construction: Choose auxiliary functions, endpoints, oscillatory test points, constants, majorants, or canonical objects so that the desired sign, bound, or existence property becomes directly verifiable.
6. Elementary algebra and order reasoning: Strengthen strict inequalities to weak ones, prove positivity or nonvanishing, clear denominators, cancel factors, rearrange identities, and combine opposing inequalities by transitivity or antisymmetry.
7. Compactness arguments: Use compactness of closed intervals or compact images to obtain boundedness, attained extrema, uniform continuity, and convergent subsequences.
8. Suprema, infima, and order completeness: Use least-upper-bound and greatest-lower-bound properties to control suprema, infima, limsups, moduli of continuity, and bounded images.
9. Restriction, extension, and transfer of regularity: Restrict continuity or differentiability to subintervals, extend a function to a larger domain when needed, and pass from continuity to integrability or from ordinary to within-domain regularity.
10. Intermediate Value Theorem: Establish continuity and an endpoint sign change or intermediate-value condition, apply the IVT on a closed interval, and exclude endpoints when strict interior membership is required.
11. Limit laws, squeezing, and uniqueness: Compose limits, pass inequalities to limits, apply the squeeze theorem, and use Hausdorff uniqueness—together with nontriviality of the relevant filter—to identify limits.
12. Difference-quotient characterization of differentiability: Express differentiability as convergence of the slope on a punctured neighborhood and use the equivalence in either direction.
13. Differential calculus rules: Apply standard derivative rules such as the chain rule, together with closure of differentiability under composition and related operations.
14. Fermat, Rolle, and Mean Value Theorems: Reduce extrema or endpoint equalities to a zero derivative, or use the Mean Value Theorem to represent an increment by an intermediate derivative and then rearrange algebraically.
15. Derivative-sign arguments: Infer monotonicity from the sign of the derivative, recover derivative inequalities from one-sided difference quotients, and use first- or second-derivative sign changes to prove local extrema or constancy.
16. Taylor expansion with remainder: Apply a Taylor theorem in remainder form, unfold the finite Taylor polynomial, and normalize factorial, power, and coefficient expressions to the desired identity.
17. Triangle-inequality and error-decomposition estimates: Use triangle and reverse-triangle inequalities, split an expression into dominant and error terms, and bound each component by uniform elementary estimates.
18. Series comparison and convergence tests: Compare terms with geometric or other summable majorants, use the term test for divergence, and apply root, ratio, alternating-series, or rearrangement theorems as appropriate.
19. Uniform convergence and the Weierstrass \(M\)-test: Produce an \(x\)-independent summable majorant to prove pointwise summability and uniform convergence, and transfer continuity or global bounds to the resulting sum.
20. Power-series radius arguments: Translate scalar coefficients to the associated formal series, compare coefficient norms, and apply Cauchy–Hadamard or radius-of-convergence comparison theorems.
21. Approximation and Archimedean error control: Invoke a density theorem to choose approximants with prescribed sup-norm error, then use the Archimedean property to select an index making that error uniformly smaller than a given \(\varepsilon\).
22. Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: Convert derivatives into integral increment formulas or differentiate parameterized integrals, using continuity and within-domain equality to transfer the result to the original function.
23. Structural properties of the integral: Use integrability together with linearity, additivity over adjacent intervals, monotonicity, constant-integral formulas, and the integral triangle inequality.
24. Integration by parts and substitution: Verify the required differentiability, continuity, and integrability hypotheses, then apply integration by parts or change of variables; for oscillatory integrals, choose bounded antiderivatives carrying a small factor.
25. Finite-sum reindexing and telescoping: Rewrite dependent or fiberwise sums using ordinary finite indices, reindex them bijectively, and cancel consecutive differences to leave only endpoint terms.
26. Partition and refinement arguments: Use ordered partition points to control subinterval lengths and mesh, construct common refinements, and decompose each coarse interval into the fine intervals assigned to it.
27. Modulus-of-continuity control of Riemann sums: Compare tags lying in a common coarse interval by the modulus of continuity, sum the resulting errors over interval lengths, and use vanishing mesh to prove Cauchy convergence and independence of partitions.
28. Sequence and completeness methods: Translate convergence and Cauchy properties into ε–\(N\) form, use completeness of \(\mathbb R\), compose with subsequences, or construct sequences approaching a point to contradict a proposed finite limit.
29. Monotone sequences, limsup, and subsequences: Use monotone convergence to an infimum or supremum, characterize convergence through equality of limsup and liminf, and extract convergent subsequences from bounded sequences.
30. Induction and well-ordering: Prove natural-number statements by strengthened induction or successor chaining, and obtain least elements of nonempty subsets of \(\mathbb N\) from the well-ordering principle.
31. Set-theoretic identities and extensionality: Prove set equalities by mutual inclusion or standard Boolean identities, and unfold proper inclusion, complements, intersections, unions, and differences.
32. Ordered-field structure and canonical constructions: Invoke standard field and order instances, use canonical witnesses such as \(0\), \(-x\), \(1\), \(x^{-1}\), or \(\sqrt{x}\), and use completeness, Archimedeanness, and rational cuts in structural characterizations of the reals.
33. Cardinality and embedding arguments: Prove uncountability or cardinal inequalities using Cantor’s theorem, diagonalization, interval cardinality, and injections; obtain equivalences from mutual embeddings via Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein.
