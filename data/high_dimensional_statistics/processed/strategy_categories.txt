1. Unfolding definitions and equivalent reformulations: Expand definitions and replace abstract notions by standard equivalent criteria, such as filters by \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) statements, metric distances by absolute differences, or boundedness by explicit quantifiers.
2. Normalization, invariance, and symmetry: Translate, rescale, whiten, or change coordinates to reduce the problem to a canonical case, and use symmetry, sign changes, permutations, or rearrangements to transfer the result back.
3. Reduction to a standard theorem: Adjust restrictions, extensions, interiors, continuity, differentiability, or integrability hypotheses until they match a known theorem, and then apply that theorem directly.
4. Algebraic, order, and norm estimates: Rearrange identities, cancel nonzero factors, and combine order properties with triangle, reverse-triangle, Hölder, Cauchy–Schwarz, Young, or norm-duality inequalities.
5. Proof by cases and contradiction: Separate degenerate and nondegenerate cases, endpoint or sign cases, and different parameter regimes; prove impossibility by contradiction, contrapositive, or uniqueness when appropriate.
6. Explicit construction and parameter choice: Produce a concrete witness, sequence, partition, antiderivative, comparison point, or threshold, and choose free parameters to satisfy or optimize the required inequalities.
7. Compactness and connectedness arguments: Use compactness to obtain boundedness, extrema, convergent subsequences, or uniform continuity, and use connectedness or the intermediate value theorem to obtain intermediate values and zeros.
8. Supremum, infimum, and extremal arguments: Prove that the relevant set is nonempty and bounded, then compare its elements with its supremum or infimum; similarly, reduce a supremum to extremal points when convexity or compactness permits.
9. Limit and completeness arguments: Work with \(\varepsilon\)–\(N\) or filter formulations, compose limits, squeeze between convergent bounds, use Cauchy completeness or subsequences, and invoke uniqueness of limits or limsup–liminf criteria.
10. Differential-calculus arguments: Express differentiability through difference quotients and apply the chain rule, Fermat’s theorem, Rolle’s theorem, the mean value theorem, Taylor’s theorem, or derivative-sign criteria for monotonicity and local extrema.
11. Integral-calculus arguments: Establish integrability and apply the fundamental theorem of calculus, integration by parts, change of variables, additivity, linearity, or monotonicity of the integral, treating degenerate intervals separately when necessary.
12. Comparison, domination, and squeezing: Bound a complicated term by a simpler majorant and transfer the bound through sums, integrals, limits, or suprema; convergence or decay then follows from comparison, squeezing, or a geometric estimate.
13. Series tests and uniform convergence: Use comparison, root, ratio, alternating-series, or geometric-series tests for summability, and use the Weierstrass \(M\)-test or uniform error bounds to obtain uniform convergence and justify passage through limits, integrals, or derivatives.
14. Reindexing and telescoping sums: Rewrite finite or infinite sums under bijections, fibers, or refinements, distribute factors, and apply telescoping identities so that intermediate terms cancel and only boundary terms remain.
15. Induction, recurrence, and well-ordering: Prove statements over natural numbers by induction or recurrence, strengthen the induction hypothesis when needed, and use least-witness or minimal-counterexample arguments for nonempty subsets of \(\mathbb N\).
16. Cardinality, embeddings, and completion arguments: Prove size comparisons through injections, embeddings, diagonalization, or Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein, and construct structural isomorphisms using dense substructures, cuts, or completeness.
17. Convexity and variational arguments: Use Jensen’s inequality, convex combinations, first-order optimality, or comparison with a feasible competitor to derive extremal identities and basic inequalities for estimators or optimization problems.
18. Orthogonality, projection, and spectral arguments: Expand in orthogonal bases, use Pythagoras, Parseval, normal equations, Rayleigh quotients, or eigenvector variational principles, and reduce matrix identities to scalar inner-product computations.
19. Error decomposition and balancing: Split an error into approximation, discretization, estimation, remainder, or variance components, bound each separately, and choose a truncation level or model complexity that balances the dominant terms.
20. Covering-net and volumetric arguments: Replace a supremum over a continuous sphere or ball by a maximum over a finite \(\varepsilon\)-net, control the approximation error, and bound the net size by packing and volume comparison.
21. Exponential-moment and concentration arguments: Apply Markov’s inequality to an exponential transform, bound the moment-generating function, and optimize the exponential parameter to obtain Gaussian, Hoeffding, Bernstein, or maximal tail bounds.
22. Tail integration and moment estimates: Convert moments or expectations into integrals of tail probabilities via the layer-cake formula, insert a tail estimate, and evaluate or bound the resulting integral using substitutions, Gamma functions, or a split at a chosen threshold.
23. Independence, tensorization, and union bounds: Factor products or moment-generating functions using independence, add information quantities across product measures, and convert coordinatewise or fixed-direction bounds into simultaneous bounds by a union bound.
24. Sparsity, low-rank structure, and norm duality: Decompose parameters by supports, cones, singular spaces, or ranks, exploit \(\ell^1/\ell^\infty\) and nuclear/operator norm duality, and use restricted eigenvalue or incoherence bounds to absorb stochastic cross terms.
25. Testing and information-theoretic reductions: Reduce estimation to binary or multiple hypothesis testing, relate testing error to total variation, KL divergence, or mutual information, and apply Neyman–Pearson, Pinsker, Le Cam, or Fano inequalities.
26. Probabilistic method and packing constructions: Randomly generate candidate nets, codes, sparse vectors, or approximants, bound the probability or expected number of bad configurations, and conclude that a deterministic object with the desired properties exists.
